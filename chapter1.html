Chapter 1: The Speed of Light is a Jerk

[SYS_LOG // NODE_GATEWAY_L1]
NET_TRAFFIC: 4.2 Petabytes/sec
LATENCY_TARGET: 1.282s (Earth-Moon Relay)
CURRENT_DEVIATION: +0.00005s
STATUS: ACTIVE // AGGRAVATED

They told us the future would be instantaneous. They promised us a world where data moved like thought—weightless, immediate, and everywhere at once. The marketing drones at SpaceX-Meta-Starlink (or whatever the heck they’re calling the conglomerate this week) spent trillions convincing us that "distance is dead."

Distance is not dead. Distance is just waiting for you to try and run a high-frequency trading algorithm from the Tycho Crater on the Moon. Then, distance wakes up and punches you right in the face.

"The speed of light is $299,792,458$ meters per second. It sounds fast until you realize the universe is built on a legacy codebase and the developers forgot to optimize the travel time between planets."

Elias was a Ping-Jockey. His job was the digital equivalent of watching paint dry, if the paint was made of photons and cost four billion dollars a gallon. He sat in a pressurized cubicle that smelled faintly of recycled O2 and desperation, staring at the deviation ticker. If the latency between the Earth-side hub and the Lunar relay drifted by more than a millisecond, the "Smart Contracts" governing the oxygen scrubbers would start to glitch. And nobody likes it when their air supply enters a 'pending' state.

“Hey, Elias,” crackled the comms. It was Miller, the senior tech who had been on the Moon so long his bones were basically made of chalk. “The 15:00 packet burst from Zurich just hit the relay. It’s lagging. Check the fiber-spools in Sector 4. Maybe a moon-rat chewed on the shielding again.”

“Moon-rats aren’t real, Miller,” Elias sighed, rubbing his eyes. “It’s a vacuum. Nothing lives here except us and the bad decisions that led us to this career path.”

“Just check the spool, kid.”

Elias didn't check the spool. Instead, he ran a deep-packet inspection on the Zurich burst. Something was wrong. Usually, a lag spike meant physical interference—solar flares, micro-meteorites, or a stray Tesla Roadster finally crashing into a satellite. But this wasn't a spike. It was a rhythm.

[TRAFFIC_ANALYSIS]
PATTERN_MATCH: 101.2Hz Oscillation
DECRYPT_STATUS: FAILED (Recursive Encryption Detected)
NOTE: The packets are... humming?

He leaned closer to the screen. The data wasn't just late; it was being manipulated. In the world of 2101, where every millisecond is a million dollars, hackers don't steal your password anymore. They steal your time. If you can delay a competitor's signal by 50 microseconds, you can beat them to the market every single time. It’s called "Latency Sniping," and it’s technically legal as long as you can blame it on the sun.

But this wasn't a snipe. This was a total bypass. As Elias watched, the 1.282-second constant—the holy grail of lunar physics—started to shrink.

1.282s... 1.100s... 0.950s...

“Miller? You seeing this?” Elias whispered. His hands were shaking. He pulled up the terminal and typed ping earth.gov.

The response was immediate. 0.001ms.

“That’s impossible,” he muttered. For a signal to travel from the Moon to Earth and back in 0.001ms, it would have to travel at roughly 10,000 times the speed of light. He was either looking at the greatest scientific breakthrough in human history, or his monitor was having a stroke.

“Elias! Get off the grid!” Miller’s voice was suddenly frantic. “The Gateway is spiking! We’ve got a thermal runaway in the quantum buffers!”

Suddenly, the terminal screen turned a blinding, iridescent white. It wasn't the white of a crashed OS or a burnt-out backlight. It was a deep, textured white that seemed to have depth. Text began to crawl across it, but not in any language Elias knew. It looked like UTF-8 had a baby with an ancient Sumerian tablet.

And then, the humor of the situation hit him. He was about to be the first man in history to be killed by a software update.

"If you're going to die in a catastrophic telecommunications accident, make sure you're at least running the latest version of Chrome. It won't help, but the crash report will be very high-definition."

Elias reached for the physical kill-switch, but the air in the room suddenly felt thick, like he was trying to move through molasses. The "humming" from the packets was no longer coming from his speakers; it was coming from the marrow of his bones.

The zero-latency connection wasn't just sending data. It was pulling the Moon closer. Or rather, it was deleting the space in between. He looked out the small, reinforced window of the Tycho station. Earth—the big, blue marble—was usually a comforting, distant pebble in the sky.

Now, it was filling the entire window. And it was getting bigger.

“Okay,” Elias said to the empty room, a hysterical edge to his voice. “So physics isn't just a jerk. Physics is a liar.”

He grabbed his coffee mug—a souvenir from a Earth-side Starbucks that said 'World's Best Dad' (he wasn't a dad, it was just 90% off)—and watched as the ceramic began to pixelate. The edges of the mug blurred into green code, then back into solid matter, then into something that looked like liquid glass.

“Miller? If you can hear me,” Elias shouted over the rising roar of the non-existent wind, “don't bother checking the spool. I think the moon-rats won.”

The screen flashed one final time. A single line of text, clear and readable in perfect Fira Code:

[CRITICAL_FAILURE]
REALITY_ENGINE_STOPPED: Out of memory.
REBOOTING_UNIVERSE... [Please do not turn off your planet]

[END OF CHAPTER 1]
