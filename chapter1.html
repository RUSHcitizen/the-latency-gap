They told us the future would be instantaneous. They promised us a world where data moved like thought—weightless, immediate, and everywhere at once. But physics doesn't care about your marketing department's promises. Physics has a speed limit, and it's a cold, hard constant: $c \approx 299,792,458$ meters per second. In the vacuum of space, that's fast. In the silicon veins of the Global Neural Grid, it’s a crawl.

Elias watched the terminal. The "Latency Gap" wasn't just a technical hurdle; it was a socio-economic canyon. The Lunar colonies were always 1.3 seconds behind Earth. Mars was anywhere from three to twenty-two minutes behind. For the elite on Earth, this was fine for streaming movies, but for the "Lag-Born"—those living on the rim—it meant being forever disconnected from the heartbeat of the Inner World. You couldn't play a real-time game, you couldn't trade on the high-frequency markets, and you certainly couldn't use a Remote-Presence Shell without the deadly "Sync-Sickness" that came from the delay between your brain's intent and the robot's action.

Elias was a "Ping-Jockey," a low-level diagnostic tech for the Node Gateway L1. His job was to monitor the fiber-optic trunks that tunneled through the crust of the moon. He spent twelve hours a day staring at green text on a black background, ensuring that the 1.282-second target was met. If it drifted to 1.283, the financial algorithms on Earth would start dropping Lunar sell-orders, and the colony’s economy would take a hit.

But tonight, the numbers weren't drifting up. They were collapsing.

[SYS_ALERT // ANOMALY_DETECTED]
PACKET_ID: #8892-OMEGA
ORIGIN: UNKNOWN // DEEP_SPACE_ARRAY_7
TIMESTAMP_SENT: 14:02:01.000
TIMESTAMP_RECV: 14:02:01.000
LATENCY_VALUE: 0.000000ms

Elias leaned in, his glasses reflecting the scrolling green code. Zero latency was mathematically impossible. It suggested that the data had arrived at the exact moment it was sent, bypassing the speed of light entirely. It suggested **Non-Locality**. It suggested that someone had figured out how to use Quantum Entanglement to send actual information—a feat that every physics textbook since 1920 said was a one-way ticket to a paradox.

He ran a traceroute. The packet didn't come from Earth. It didn't even come from the Lunar hub. It seemed to materialize inside the buffer of his own machine. "A ghost in the shell," he muttered, his pulse quickening. If he reported this, the Corporate Security teams would be here in minutes. They’d wipe the drive, sign him to a non-disclosure agreement with a heavy-duty legal muzzle, and he’d never see the light of day again.

But the packet was still there, sitting in the cache like a ticking time bomb. It was encrypted with a 4096-bit key that used a cipher he didn't recognize. It wasn't standard AES or RSA. It looked... organic. The code segments shifted when he tried to highlight them, like oil on water.

Elias checked the door to the server room. The mag-lock was green. He was alone for another twenty minutes. He bypassed the main firewall—a move that would cost him his job if he were caught—and opened a private terminal. He began to feed the ghost-packet into his personal decompiler.

"Talk to me," he whispered. "How did you get here without traveling?"

The screen didn't display code. It displayed a map. Not a map of the moon, or the solar system, but a map of the *latency itself*. It showed the gaps between the nodes not as distances, but as physical tunnels. It was a blueprint for a highway that didn't exist in three-dimensional space.

Suddenly, the terminal chirped. A new log entry appeared, but it wasn't a system message. It was a line of plain text.

> ELIAS, STOP LOOKING AT THE SPEEDOMETER. THE ROAD IS GONE.

The lights in the room flickered. The hum of the server racks changed pitch, rising to a scream. Elias realized with a jolt of terror that the "Zero Latency" wasn't a signal from someone else. It was a leak. Something from the outside was trying to fold the space between his terminal and... wherever *they* were.

He reached for the emergency kill-switch, but his hand froze. On the screen, the map was expanding. It was showing his own heartbeat, synced perfectly with the blinking cursor. 0.000ms delay. He wasn't just watching the data anymore. He was part of the circuit.
