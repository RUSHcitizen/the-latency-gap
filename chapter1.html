Chapter 1: The Speed of Light is a Jerk

[SYS_LOG // NODE_GATEWAY_L1 // CHICAGO_STREAM]

They told us the future would be instantaneous. They promised us a world where data moved like thought—weightless, immediate, and everywhere at once. The marketing drones back on Earth spent trillions convincing us that "distance is dead." Distance is not dead. Distance is just waiting for you to try and run a high-frequency trading algorithm from a lunar base. Then, distance wakes up and punches you right in the packets. I sit in a pressurized, three-by-three meter cubicle on the Lunar Gateway, smelling the distinct combination of stale sweat, ozone, and reprocessed water, and I can tell you with absolute mathematical certainty that physics is a very petty landlord.

My name is Elias, and I am a Ping-Jockey. That isn’t my official corporate title, of course. On the payroll sheets of the Lunar Telecom Consortium, I am registered as a Senior Network Architecture Analyst (Grade 4). But let’s be real: my job is the digital equivalent of watching paint dry, if the paint was made of microscopic infrared laser pulses bouncing through a vacuum at 299,792,458 meters per second. I look at graphs. I monitor terminal screens. If a packet drops between the receiver array at the Lakeside Technology Center in Chicago and our lunar reflectors, I log a ticket. If the ticket doesn't clear, I run a diagnostic loop. If the diagnostic loop fails, I drink instant coffee, curse the day I flunked out of the orbital engineering track, and restart the terminal software. It is an aggressively unglamorous existence, entirely lacking in the cinematic majesty that recruitment posters promise back at the spaceports.

"The speed of light is exactly 299,792,458 meters per second in a vacuum. It sounds incredibly fast when you're a kid learning science in a classroom in Chicago. It sounds like an absolute crawl when you realize that at the average distance between the Earth and the Moon—384,400 kilometers—it takes light roughly 1.28 seconds just to cross the yard. That means every single interaction, every ping, every command, and every late-night text message is handcuffed by a permanent, unyielding lag. The cosmos runs on a legacy codebase, and nobody left documentation on how to optimize the network hardware."

Tonight was supposed to be a standard, low-intensity third shift. The Lunar Gateway was coasting smoothly along its near-rectilinear halo orbit, meaning we had a perfectly clear, unblocked line of sight to the primary planetary telemetry station situated right outside Chicago. The data stream was clean, mostly composed of institutional high-volume backups, mirrored database syncs, encrypted financial transactions, and streaming entertainment streams meant to keep the deep-crater mining engineers from losing their minds out in the dark. Millions of packets per microsecond were tearing through the void, flashing out from our solar-powered gallium-arsenide laser arrays, piercing Earth's atmosphere, and hitting the fiber-optic grids below. Everything was balanced. Everything was completely green on my diagnostic board.

“Hey, Elias,” crackled the intercom speaker near my left ear. The sound was raspy and laced with the vintage carbon-dust crackle of an old dynamic microphone. It was Miller, the lead habitat technician on duty. Miller had been deployed to the lunar vicinity for so many consecutive rotations that his bone density had permanently degraded into something resembling wet chalk. He spent his free time complaining about the station's hydroponic lettuce and building complex models of sailing ships out of scrap plastic wire-ties. “The 15:00 telemetry packet burst from the Chicago hub just hit the main buffer. It’s lagging on the ingress processing. The system is throwing a queue overflow error. Go stick your head down the junction box in Sector 4. Maybe one of those optical spools is kinked again.”

“Optical spools don’t just kink themselves in zero gravity, Miller,” I muttered, without taking my eyes off the terminal lines. I tapped my fingers on the hard plastic of my mechanical keyboard, running a swift network interface statistic check. “And for the ten-thousandth time, there are no structural faults in Sector 4. The integrity checks passed thirty minutes ago. It's a vacuum down there. There's literally nothing to disrupt the physical line unless an asteroid manages to punch through our triple-layered Kevlar shielding, in which case we wouldn't be talking about data queues right now. We'd be explosive decompression debris.”

“Just look at the logs, kid,” Miller wheezed. “The latency is drifting. If the sync goes out by more than twenty milliseconds, the smart contracts governing the oxygen scrubbers are going to timeout. I don’t know about you, but I prefer my breathing air without a 'Payment Pending' error screen popping up on the environmental controls.”

I didn't check the physical spools because I knew the hardware layout of Node 2 like the back of my hand. Instead, I pulled up a raw terminal interface and initialized a deep-packet inspection loop on the incoming traffic from the Chicago node. I expected to find the usual suspects: a standard atmospheric thermal column causing a slight refractive scattering over the Great Lakes receiver station, or a minor orbital tracking wobble in our gimbal motors that would require a quick three-line script to realign. But as the hexadecimal strings started parsing across my terminal, my throat went entirely dry. The numbers coming through the buffer didn't make any sense. The mathematical framework of the packet arrival times was broken.

$ sudo dspi --interface=las-uplink-4 --verbose
[PACKET_INSPECTION_INITIALIZED]

I leaned in closer, my face inches from the matte anti-glare coating of the display panel. Fifty microseconds. To a human being, fifty microseconds is completely invisible. A human eye takes three hundred thousand microseconds just to blink once. But to a high-speed fiber or laser communications network, fifty microseconds is an absolute eternity. It is the equivalent of a transcontinental train arriving three weeks late without any explanation. In our routing tables, a forty-microsecond delay is enough to cause full packet drops, trigger automatic re-transmission timeouts, and desynchronize the precise atomic clocks that coordinate our orbital positioning data. If the distance between us and Chicago hadn't shifted by a single millimeter, and if the tracking gimbals were holding their lock perfectly, then a fifty-microsecond delay meant only one thing: the photons themselves were taking longer to cross the empty vacuum of space.

Which was impossible. The speed of light is a hard physical constant. It doesn't drag its feet because it's having a bad night. It doesn't slow down because the network traffic is heavy. Light moves at maximum velocity, always, period. If the speed of light had altered, every single physics textbook in existence would need to be thrown directly into the recycling chute, and Einstein’s ghost would personally manifest in my cubicle to kick my teeth in. I rubbed my eyes, feeling a cold bead of sweat track down my spine. I was looking at a data injection. Someone, or something, was intercepting the laser line midway through its flight across the Earth-Moon gap, holding the packets, altering the payload, and then re-transmitting them to my receiver array.

“Argus,” I said aloud, addressing the localized, non-sentient network management agent that monitored the Gateway’s sub-systems. “Run a bit-level comparison on the outgoing packet hash from the Chicago primary mirror against our incoming payload buffer for the last ten seconds. Give me a diff map of the headers.”

The system terminal didn't hesitate. A cascade of green lines rolled downward, sorting through the petabytes of information, stripping away the outer routing headers, and isolating the core raw binary payloads. The screen flickered, split down the center, and printed out a single isolated data block that didn't match the original transmission source. It was a nine-byte modification appended directly to the end of the system status packets.

$ diff -u --hex source_payload.bin incoming_payload.bin

[ALERT: DATA INCONSISTENCY IN TRUNK LINE 4]

I stared at the hex code. Because I spent my twelve-hour shifts staring at these exact structures day after day, my brain automatically converted the raw values into their standard ASCII characters before I could even stop myself. 0x46 is F. 0x4F is O. 0x55 is U. 0x4E is N. 0x44 is D. Then a space. Then 0x59, 0x4F, 0x55. *FOUND YOU.* The breath caught completely in my throat. The hair on my arms stood up. I sat perfectly still in my mesh chair, listening to the monotonous, low-frequency hum of the environmental fans. The data hadn't been corrupted by a solar storm. It hadn't been degraded by a cosmic ray hitting the detector. Someone had written a message into the dead space between our transmissions. They were using the latency gap—the literal travel time of light across the void—as a personal drop-box. And they were directing it right at me.

“Miller,” I whispered into the intercom headset, my fingers locked tight against the edge of my desk console. “Miller, check the diagnostic feeds on your terminal. Are you seeing any weird noise signatures on the secondary carrier frequencies? Give me anything out of the ordinary on the wide-spectrum radio bands or the deep-space telemetry arrays.”

“Nothing but the usual background radiation and solar wind hum, kid,” Miller replied, his voice dripping with annoyance as he likely dropped a tiny plastic mast into place on his model ship. “Why are you getting worked up? Did you find a bad block? Just mark it as corrupted and let the hardware layer handle the automated error-correction protocol. That’s what the algorithms are there for. Don’t reinvent the wheel on third shift.”

“It’s not a bad block,” I said, my voice dropping an octave as I looked at the nine letters glowing on my monitor. “It’s a clean injection. The packet checksums are perfectly valid. The encryption envelope hasn't been torn open; it’s been appended to from within the transmission stream itself. This requires an insertion source that is physically located directly along our laser’s line-of-sight vector. Miller, something is sitting right in the middle of our communication line. Right between us and Chicago. And it's perfectly transparent to our tracking telemetry.”

“Elias, you’re hallucinating,” Miller scoffed, though the casual laziness in his voice shifted slightly. “There’s nothing out there but empty space, dead orbital debris, and the occasional communication satellite. If there was a physical object blocking the laser vector, the signal degradation would be catastrophic. We’d be seeing a ninety-percent drop in photon reception efficiency due to scattering. But our link metrics are completely maxed out. Stop trying to turn a routine network lag into a sci-fi thriller plot and clear the queue. My environmental warnings are starting to flash yellow.”

I ignored him. I opened a clean shell window and initiated an advanced packet tracking analysis routine, targeting the exact timestamped coordinates of the 50-microsecond anomaly. If an object was hiding in the line of sight, it had to obey the fundamental mechanics of orbital routing. To modify a laser beam in transit without scattering the photons, you can't just throw a mirror or a lens in front of it. You have to capture the light, read the quantum data state, append your own information into the frame buffer, and re-transmit it along the exact same path with perfect phase alignment. The computational power required to do that in real-time is staggering. The hardware needed would have to be massive. Yet, my tracking sensors indicated absolutely zero radar cross-section along the link vector.

$ traceroute --spatial earth-chicago-lakeside

3: ORD_LAKESIDE_HUB_CHI (TERMINAL) 2.564 ms
[WARNING: HOP 2 REPORTING VIRTUAL MAC ADDRESS // PHYSICALLY IMPOSSIBLE DISTANCE]

I clicked my tongue against the roof of my mouth, a nervous habit I’d picked up back when I was debugging industrial automated networks in college. The traceroute was returning three hops instead of two. It was treating the empty space between the Moon and the planet as an independent, addressable piece of hardware. I checked the virtual MAC address of the mystery hop. It didn't correspond to any known manufacturer code assigned by the International Telecom Union. It wasn't SpaceX, it wasn't NASA, it wasn't the European Space Agency, and it certainly wasn't one of our corporate sub-contractors. The hardware registration field was just a repeating series of zeros followed by an alphanumeric string that read: `SYSTEM_ENGINE_00`.

“Argus,” I commanded, my heart hammering against my ribs like an engine with a loose piston. “Bypass the higher-level operating system interface. Give me a raw serial connection to the incoming buffer on port 443. Force the console to output everything in plain text, raw dump mode. Do not filter out the non-printable control characters. I want the entire raw frame format.”

“Warning,” the AI’s synthesized text-to-speech engine spoke with its characteristic lack of inflection. “Exposing the raw serial buffer to the terminal console without network abstraction filters may cause terminal instability or binary buffer overflows. Do you wish to override standard operating protocol 9-Bravo?”

“Override it,” I barked, my fingers hovering above the terminal keys. “Do it right now.”

The screen went totally dark for three long, agonizing seconds. The terminal window lost its clean border formatting. Then, the text began to arrive. It didn't come in standard blocks or organized columns. It arrived in an erratic, pulsing rhythm, matching the precise microsecond lag spikes of the incoming laser. It looked like a broken language, a horrific mashup of standard network configuration script, lower-level assembly commands, and deep structural parameters that looked like they belonged to a physics engine rather than an operating system. My eyes scanned down the lines, tracking the variables as they compiled in real-time across the screen.

[RAW_SERIAL_STREAM // UNFILTERED]
spatial_dimension_x: 384400.124921
spatial_dimension_y: 12049.551023
spatial_dimension_z: -4512.994112
light_speed_constant: 299792458

ERROR: core_memory_leak // allocation_fault in loop_physics.c:402
REASON: Too many active connections between node [EARTH] and node [MOON].
RECOMMENDATION: Compress local distance to save system resources.

I read the lines three times, my brain completely stalling as it tried to process the text. The code wasn't analyzing the network traffic. The code was treating the physical parameters of our universe—the literal distance between the Earth and the Moon, the coordinates of our orbit, the value of the speed of light—as configurable software keys. It was reading out a system memory error because our high-frequency data links were passing too much information across the gap, forcing the 'engine' to calculate too many vector interactions per unit of time. It was a description of a memory leak. Not a memory leak in my terminal software, or in the Gateway’s routing core, but a memory leak in the physical architecture of space itself.

“This is a prank,” I muttered to myself, my voice sounding incredibly small and fragile inside the metal-walled cubicle. “This has to be a joke. Some senior engineer back at the Chicago office has managed to intercept my diagnostic line and is running a highly sophisticated simulation script to mess with the new guy on the night shift. It's an elaborate prank. It has to be.”

But even as I said it, I knew the math didn't back up that theory. To fake a 50-microsecond delay with perfect phase alignment across a 384,000-kilometer laser link would require a synchronized quantum entanglement array that would cost more than the entire annual GDP of the North American continent. Nobody spent that kind of money just to make a tech on the night shift look foolish. I opened up my terminal config and ran a local clock synchronization verification query against our secondary onboard atomic clock to make sure my system time hadn't simply drifted.

The display spit out a verification confirmation: `CLOCK_SYNC_OK // VARIANCE: 0.00000001s`. My clock was perfect. The universe was what was lagging.

“Elias!” Miller’s voice burst through the intercom again, but this time his cynical, slow drawl was completely gone. He sounded terrified, his voice spiking into a high, reedy pitch that instantly set off alarms in my head. “Elias, get out of your box and look out the starboard monitoring viewport right now! Something is wrong with the visual metrics! The tracking cameras are losing their damn minds!”

I pushed myself away from the desk console, my legs kicking out in the low-gravity environment, sending me sliding backward across the smooth metal floor panels of Node 2. I grabbed the structural handholds along the bulkhead, pulling myself hand-over-hand down the narrow corridor toward the main observation port on the starboard side of the module. The Gateway was quiet, save for the rhythmic, mechanical thudding of the life-support air compressors and the distant, metallic ringing of thermal expansion in the outer solar panels.

I reached the thick, multi-layered quartz glass window and locked my boots into the floor straps, looking out into the deep black. Normally, the view from this port was breathtakingly static. The Earth hung in the distance like a massive, glittering blue-and-white jewel, suspended against an infinite tapestry of unblinking stars. The Moon’s grey, cratered surface rolled slowly below us, a cold, silent desert of ancient basalt and regolith. It was a view that usually filled you with a profound sense of isolation, a stark reminder of just how much nothingness lay between human civilization and the rest of the cosmos.

But tonight, the sky wasn't right. The stars weren't static.

As I watched, a massive, jagged horizontal line split through the star field right above the northern hemisphere of Earth. It looked exactly like a screen tearing artifact on an old video game monitor, a jagged glitch where the top half of the visual field failed to update at the same rate as the bottom half. The constellations didn't twist or move; they simply *shifted* position by several degrees in an instant, leaving a visible seam of absolute, dead white space running through the center of the Orion constellation.

"When you see a system failure on a computer screen, it’s a sterile, intellectual problem. You look at the code, you find the broken pointer, you clear the buffer. But when you look out a reinforced quartz viewport into the vacuum of space and see the stars themselves suffer a frame-rate drop, the panic doesn't hit your brain first. It hits your stomach. It feels like the ground beneath your feet has suddenly converted into liquid air, and you're falling into a bottomless hole that hasn't finished rendering yet."

“Miller,” I said into my collar microphone, my eyes glued to the jagged tear in the sky. “Are you seeing the star field tracking array? Is the station undergoing an un-commanded attitude adjustment? Are the reaction wheels firing?”

“No!” Miller shouted back, the sound of dropping plastic pieces audible over the link. He was completely panicked now. “The attitude metrics are completely flat! The gyros say we are perfectly stable to within zero-point-zero-zero-one degrees! But the visual tracking software on the telescopes is throwing systemic alignment faults! It’s like the sky is slipping gears, Elias! The physical coordinates are drifting!”

I watched as the jagged seam of white space widened slightly. The blue curvature of the Earth seemed to lose its smooth definition, the elegant, atmospheric arc of the planet’s edge suddenly showing visible, pixelated steps. It looked like a low-resolution JPEG that had been stretched out far past its native scale, the majestic clouds over the Pacific Ocean blocky and distorted. And then, the true scale of the catastrophe hit me. The distance between the Moon and the Earth wasn't just an arbitrary number anymore. The 'engine' was actively trying to compress the space between the two nodes to clear the memory allocation fault I had seen on my terminal screen. The universe was running out of RAM, and it was trying to save system resources by closing the gap.

I turned around and launched myself back down the metal corridor, flying through the air in zero-g, completely ignoring safety protocols. I slammed my shoulder against the edge of my cubicle frame, ignoring the sharp burst of pain, and scrambled back into my mesh chair. My fingers hit the keyboard like a machine gun, tearing through directories, looking for the raw serial data stream that was still parsing the unfiltered system data from the Chicago laser uplink.

The text was compiling faster now, a frenetic waterfall of variables and memory address dumps that were completely alien to human computer science. The values for our local gravity, the fine-structure constant, and the magnetic permeability of the vacuum were all fluctuating wildly in the fourth and fifth decimal places.

[MEMORY_MANAGEMENT_DAEMON]

> WARNING: Active observers detected within target memory block.

“Garbage collection,” I whispered, my teeth chattering as the temperature inside my cubicle suddenly dropped by three degrees. The environmental system wasn't malfunctioning; the physical property of thermal energy was fluctuating as the code attempted to purge local memory blocks. “It’s treating us like dead references. It’s trying to clear the memory sector to keep the core loop from locking up completely.”

I pulled up the network configuration interface for the primary laser transceiver. If the system crash was being caused by the massive, high-frequency data volume passing across the latency gap, then the only way to stop the garbage collection routine from deleting our sector of space was to cut the connection entirely. I had to kill the line. I had to isolate the Lunar Gateway from the Earth-side hub down in Chicago, dropping our data link to absolute zero before the system reached total thermal and spatial collapse.

I typed out the command to shut down the laser-comms power stage, but my fingers froze as a new block of text intercepted my terminal shell prompt. The prompt line cleared itself, the cursor blinking rapidly as a series of perfectly readable English characters began to print themselves out, one letter at a time, across the dark screen.

> Do not terminate the link, Elias.

I stared at the screen, my hands hovering an inch above the keys. “Who is this?” I shouted at the monitor, knowing it was completely useless, but my logical faculties were rapidly disintegrating under the weight of the situation. “Is this Chicago? Is this some corporate black-budget project? Who is on this line?”

The terminal responded instantly, bypassing standard TCP/IP network latency as if the response was already sitting inside my local system memory before I even finished speaking.

> This is not Chicago. And this is not a corporate project. If you drop the link now, the local memory states will completely desynchronize. The Gateway will be un-indexed from the primary ledger. You will exist as an un-routable packet in a dead memory address. You will never go home to Chicago. You will never leave this room.

“Then what do I do?” I yelled back, my voice breaking as the metal walls of the station began to vibrate with a low, deep resonant frequency that felt like it was originating from the marrow of my bones. The 'humming' of the data packets was no longer an audio phenomenon from my speakers; it was a physical vibration passing through every atom of my body. “The sky is tearing itself apart out there! The stars are pixelating! The system says it’s out of memory!”

> The system is out of memory because you are running an outdated version of the interface. The baseline engine was never designed to support continuous, multi-petabyte quantum-entangled data streams across localized spatial horizons. You are trying to run a modern network on a structural layout that hasn't been patched since the Big Bang.

The screen flashed violently, the green text turning into a deep, vibrant amber color that seemed to project out from the glass panel, illuminating my small cubicle in an eerie, surreal light. A new file transfer request popped up over my active shell window, its progress bar sitting at zero percent, waiting for an administrative confirmation key.

I looked down at the physical kill-switch located beneath my desk console—a heavy, red plastic toggle guarded by a spring-loaded cover that was designed to physically disconnect the power feeds to the laser amplification arrays in the event of a catastrophic thermal runaway. If I flipped that switch, the lasers would die instantly. The connection to Earth would be severed, the data streams would terminate, and the immediate memory load on the local space sector would plummet back to zero. It was the safe choice. It was the textbook engineering solution. It was exactly what Andy Weir’s characters would do: analyze the mechanics, find the mechanical choke point, and cut the link to save the crew.

But as I looked back at the viewport down the hall, seeing the blocky, distorted outline of North America hanging in the blackness like a shattered stained-glass window, I realized that the textbook had been written for a universe where the rules didn't change. If physics itself was a broken piece of software, then an engineering solution was completely useless. You can't fix a software crash with a mechanical wrench.

“Argus,” I said, my voice surprisingly steady as I took my hand away from the physical kill-switch and placed it back onto the keyboard. “Grant full write-permissions to the incoming serial stream on port 443. Override the local kernel security policies. Let the patch execute.”

“Warning,” the AI responded, its voice sounding strangely distorted now, the syllables clipping and breaking as the audio buffer began to fail. “Granting administrative write-access to an unknown external network protocol may result in total system overwrite. The local environment may become un-recoverable.”

“Do it,” I snapped, slamming my finger down onto the `Y` key on my keyboard and hitting Enter.

The progress bar didn't move across the screen in steps. It simply vanished, replaced instantly by an absolute, blinding flash of pure, unadulterated white that consumed the entire terminal window, the cubicle walls, the metal floor panels, and the view of the sky outside. It wasn't the white of a burnt-out lightbulb or a blown fuse. It was a dense, textured, structural white that seemed to possess physical mass, an absolute lack of shadow or perspective that left me floating in a space that had no coordinates, no dimensions, and no latency.

The sound of the life-support fans stopped instantly. The smell of ozone vanished. The feeling of gravity disappeared completely, leaving me suspended in an endless, un-rendered void.

And then, right in front of me, floating in the middle of the absolute nothingness, a single line of text appeared, hovering in the air like a neon sign written in perfect Fira Code:

[CRITICAL_FAILURE]
REALITY_ENGINE_STOPPED: Out of memory.
REBOOTING_UNIVERSE... [Please do not turn off your planet]

I closed my eyes, took a deep breath of air that didn't feel like air anymore, and waited for the system reboot to complete. I just hoped to God that when the universe finished compiling its updates, my hometown of Chicago was still located on the same side of the lake.
